Music is a tool for emotional regulation: people use it to feel, and to change how they feel, with the ear and with the whole body. The means of making it keep changing, from players in a room to studios, software and now agents, but only the person listening feels what it does.

In the 1970s and 1980s, the great recording studios were among the most expensive rooms in the world. A console the size of a car, a wall of tape machines, an echo chamber, a staff of engineers and assistants, session players on call: if you wanted a record that sounded like a record, you rented the room. Careers were made in those rooms, and the Hit Factory in New York was among the most famous of them. And then, within a generation, the business was in retreat, and nobody attacked it. The Hit Factory [closed in 2005](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2005-02-04/new-yorks-hit-factory-studio-to-close/630954), and the report of its closing named the cause in so many words: "the rise of inexpensive digital audio workstation equipment has allowed high-quality recording to be accomplished in virtually any environment by non-professional recordists." EMI [closed Olympic Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Studios) in London in 2009, and in 2010 it was reported to have put even [Abbey Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey_Road_Studios) up for sale; after a public campaign it kept the studio, and the British government listed the building to protect it from major alteration. In 2020, the Grammy for Album of the Year went to a record produced in a [small bedroom studio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/When_We_All_Fall_Asleep,_Where_Do_We_Go%3F) in Los Angeles, in an off-the-shelf DAW.

The mistake is to think the studios sold rooms, or tape, or consoles. They sold **the finished record**: the best way anyone had found to turn a musical idea into music other people could hear the way it was meant. When software served the same want far more cheaply, beginning with [Pro Tools](https://www.mixonline.com/technology/1991-digidesign-pro-tools-383616) in 1991, the want quietly took the shorter road. Other forces pushed too: Manhattan rents, the collapse of record sales after file-sharing, labels in debt. But the decisive fact outlived all of them. A cheaper path to the finished record had appeared, and the consoles did not lose a fight. They were bypassed, and then redrawn, knob for knob, inside the software that replaced them.

That last detail is where this essay begins. The want is the constant and the means keep dying, as I argued in [The Selection Pressure](/posts/the_selection_pressure). The DAW did not abolish the studio; it shrank the studio to fit on a laptop and kept the classical studio idea at its centre. Now it faces the pressure it once applied.

Imagine hearing a song and wishing the bass would wait a little longer before it returns. The intention takes a second to form. Realizing it can take an evening: find the source, separate the parts, learn an editor, rebuild the passage, render it, compare it with the original, and hope you can find the file next week. The DAW is the most capable musical instrument ever built, and it still charges that evening as the price of the second. The reason is the idea it was built on. The classical studio idea organizes music around jobs: recording, editing, mixing, each with its own machine and its own specialist. The oldest instruments are organized around the person holding them: a gesture goes in, a sound comes out, and the ear decides. The DAW took the jobs, and with them the rule that you learn the operation before you are allowed the intention. So the contest that matters is not between applications. It is over how far a person must travel between a musical intention and its realization, and what carries them across.

My thesis is simple. **The DAW of the future will not be a better copy of the classical studio. It will be an instrument built around the player, and played interactively**: you ask, hear, adjust and continue, while an agent works on the same editable music through the same commands, the instrument supplies the senses, the work keeps its memory, and your ear keeps the verdict. I am also betting on who that agent will be: not a special music program, but the same kind of general model that now writes software, with sound generators, written in code or built as neural models, as its tools. Music shares a good part of what made coding agents work, and adds a dimension code does not have: sound, and an ear to judge it.

The reported numbers show what abundance looks like. By one industry count, about [**106,000**](https://musically.com/2026/01/15/5-1tn-annual-music-streams-but-120-5m-tracks-had-10-or-fewer/) new tracks were delivered to streaming services every day in 2025, up from 99,000 the year before, and by September 2026 the same count was [**close to 150,000**](https://musically.com/2026/09/17/150000-new-tracks-are-uploaded-to-music-services-every-day/). One large streaming service reported that fully AI-generated tracks made up [**more than half**](https://newsroom-deezer.com/2026/07/ai-music-exceeds-50-percent-daily-uploads-deezer/) of its daily uploads by mid-2026, while drawing only **1–3%** of its streams. Of the roughly 253 million tracks on streaming services at the end of 2025, about [**88%**](https://musically.com/2026/01/15/5-1tn-annual-music-streams-but-120-5m-tracks-had-10-or-fewer/) were reportedly played fewer than a thousand times that year.

Making a finished-sounding track has never been cheaper, and getting strangers to hear one has never been harder. This essay is not about winning that contest. It is about the other end: music for you, music that fits you now, carries your own choices and absorbs you enough that you come back and play it again. When generic tracks cost nothing, what keeps its value is the specific, the thing you meant, and only control over the sound delivers it. The next instrument will be built there. To see its shape, start with the person, not the software.

## What people want from music does not change

Someone plays a familiar song after a difficult day. Someone else chooses it for a friend's wedding. A third person hears a phrase and begins answering it on an instrument. The recording may be identical; the intentions differ. People use music above all to feel and to change how they feel, and also to express what they cannot easily say, to remember, to belong, to exercise taste, and sometimes to build a skill over years. **Human wants change slowest.** People danced to a beat long before anyone could record one, and the want has outlived every way of making the beat: the drum, the dance band, the record, the DJ and the drum machine each changed only how it was made.

From those wants come three needs an instrument can serve: music that fits me now; music in which I recognize my own choices; and music through which another person answers me. Only the third needs another person, and only when someone actually wants to respond.

Two cautions keep this honest. Listening is a complete destination, not a failed attempt to create. And effort is not always a cost: people avoid the effort that stands between them and the music, and seek the effort that is the music, such as practising a rhythm or finding a sound by ear. A good instrument removes the first kind and protects the second.

People also learn the way they always have: by asking, trying and hearing an answer. Most of what humans have ever learned came from interacting with the world, not from being taught: finding food, reading the weather, making tools and staying alive were learned by doing, and schools are a recent invention in a species [about 300,000 years old](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-sapiens). Young children are the clearest case. In recordings of everyday family conversation, young children asked [about 76 questions an hour](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ835244) that sought information, and when an answer did not come, they asked again. A tool that demands the manual before the first sound works against that grain; a tool you can ask, try, hear and continue with works with it.

With agents, learning music becomes easier and more fun, on a single principle: it becomes interactive. You ask, hear the answer in the music itself, and ask again. The fear that help will stop people learning is old. In April 1986, mathematics teachers from across the United States [picketed](https://www.csmonitor.com/1986/0509/dcalc-f.html) a meeting of the National Council of Teachers of Mathematics, which had called for calculators at every grade. The calculator did not end the want to understand numbers; it changed how people reach it. The hows change, and the wants stay.

That is the measure every tool in this essay is held to. Technology does not change what people want; it changes which wants have become affordable and which costs are left standing. For a generation, the tool that decided that for recorded music has been the DAW.

## The DAW carries the classical studio

Instruments are sound generators, and music has grown with them. For most of history the generator was a body you could hold: two strings stretched over wood, a skin over a drum, a column of air. Electricity added the pickup and the amplifier, then circuits that made sound with no string at all, and the synthesizer turned a sound into a set of settings. Techno, built from drum machines, sequencers and synthesizers, is where that line now pushes the frontier. The latest [annual report](https://www.beatportal.com/articles/1386171-ims-business-report-2026-global-dance-music-industry-now-worth-15.1-billion) on the electronic music business reportedly described 2025's sound as "harder and faster", with uploads of one hard techno style up 83%. I think techno is now doing what EDM did a decade ago: pulling the mainstream toward a sound that machines make, while rap, the dominant sound of the last decade, loses momentum. In October 2025, for the first time since 1990, the top 40 of the main US singles chart [held no rap song](https://www.billboard.com/music/rb-hip-hop/no-rap-songs-hot-100-top-40-first-time-since-1990-1236100625/), helped by a change in the chart's rules. And the instrument techno is made on is the DAW. That is why I use the two words interchangeably: the DAW is the latest instrument, the one on which most new music is now made.

Tools also acquire meanings their makers never planned. Roland sold the [TB-303](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roland_TB-303) from 1981, for US$395, as a "computerised bass machine" to replace the bass guitar. Its sound was too unlike a bass guitar for the musicians it was made for, and it was discontinued in 1984. Second-hand units became cheap, and in Chicago producers found that turning its filter and resonance controls while a pattern played gave a squelching, liquid sound no bass guitar could make; [Acid Tracks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_Tracks), released in 1987, started acid house with it. Because the sound lived in a handful of settings, it could be copied exactly, and Roland and other makers have since released clones and emulations that reproduce it from the same controls. A falling price put the machine in new hands, and its new players, not its maker, decided what it was for.

The DAW, though, is an instrument built on the classical studio idea. Its screens copy the studio's rooms and jobs, recording, editing, mixing and effects, each with its own controls to learn. Some DAWs have bent the idea: [Ableton Live](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ableton_Live) was designed as an instrument for live performance, [FL Studio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FL_Studio) began as a drum machine, and [trackers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_tracker) never imitated tape at all. They made performing and sketching feel more like playing. None of them changed the rule that you learn the operation first.

That inheritance was a triumph. Recording made a performance repeatable. Multitrack made its parts revisable. Software put the whole construction on a personal computer, so that today you can generate and produce whatever sound you like, wherever you are, at a cost that has fallen by orders of magnitude in a century. But what software collapsed was the cost of equipment, not the cost of knowing. A complete DAW now comes free with every Mac and runs free in a browser; knowing which of its controls to touch, and when, still takes most people years of practice. That is the distance between operation and intention, and the classical studio idea cannot close it, because it is made of operations.

The history of music technology is a sequence of such partial victories. Notation let musical instructions travel without the performer; literacy, teaching and publishers remained the gates. Recording let people hear music without its players in the room; financing, manufacture and promotion became the gates. MIDI, agreed in 1983, let performance data travel between machines from different makers, but it carried the instructions, not the music. Each wave put a new power in people's hands and left a gate standing.

## A chain bought in layers

Look at what an electronic producer actually pays for, and the DAW turns out to be one link in a longer chain:

1. **the engine**: the synthesizers that make sound;
2. **the content**: presets, samples, loops and patterns, per genre;
3. **access**: libraries that supply and search sounds on demand;
4. **know-how**: lessons, and other people's project files;
5. **assembly**: turning parts into a track, alone or with others;
6. **release**: labels, streaming services and direct sales;
7. **performance**: the DJs, clubs and festivals where music meets a room.

Each layer has its own seller and is someone's livelihood: instrument makers, sound designers, pack makers, tutors, engineers. **The producer pays twice for every layer: once in money, and once in learning.**

The DAW sits at the assembly layer, the workbench where parts bought elsewhere are put together. That is why it has been so stable for so long: it is the centre of a chain whose other links it does not own.

Integration alone is not the answer, and neither is price. Free, integrated workstations already exist at scale: BandLab, a free studio for the browser and the phone with loops and collaboration built in, passed [100 million users](https://musically.com/2024/03/25/music-creation-firm-bandlab-says-it-has-more-than-100m-users/) in 2024. What would be new is an instrument that joins the layers *and acts on the work itself*. Where the cost now sits decides whether that is worth building.

## Where the bottleneck moves

Each wave above left a gate standing, and the rent moved to it. That is the law of The Selection Pressure: when one cost falls, the rent relocates to whichever cost has not yet fallen. In recorded music the bottleneck has already moved four times, and a fifth move is under way:

| What was scarce | What made it cheap | Whose leverage fell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The players | Synthesizers, drum machines and samplers, in the 1980s | Session musicians |
| The room: console, tape, engineers | Recording software on personal computers, from 1991 | Studio owners |
| Delivery | Digital distribution and streaming | Pressing plants and distributors |
| The sounds | Sample packs and preset libraries, now generative models | Sound designers and pack makers |
| The operation | Agents that work the DAW while you ask, hear and adjust | Years of DAW skill, and the courses that sell it |

The last row is the one moving now. For thirty years the DAW's real price was the years it took to learn. An agent you can talk to while it works removes most of that price, the same shift programming has just been through.

When sound and operation are both cheap, three costs remain for a person making music:

- **Saying what you mean.** No language describes a sound precisely enough to ask for exactly the one in your head. Musicians point instead: they hum, they play, they bring a reference track.
- **Hearing whether it worked.** A listener can tell in seconds whether something works, but every change an agent makes still has to be heard, in context, and the instrument should take you straight to what changed.
- **Making it yours, and keeping it.** Knowing which choices were yours, returning to the work, and tracing where each sound came from.

Attention stays scarce as well. The upload figures above are what abundance looks like, and one streaming service has claimed that up to [**85%**](https://newsroom-deezer.com/2026/07/ai-music-exceeds-50-percent-daily-uploads-deezer/) of the streams that fully AI-generated tracks received in 2025 were fraudulent. But winning strangers' attention is the release's problem, not the instrument's. An instrument is judged by how much of the three remaining costs it removes, and the most visible answer to abundance removes almost none of them.

## The prompt is not an instrument

That answer is the song generator: type a description, receive a finished track. It is a remarkable technology, and the record industry has decided to live with it. Universal Music settled its lawsuit against Udio in [October 2025](https://musically.com/2025/10/30/umg-settles-udio-lawsuit-companies-plan-new-ai-music-service-together/) and agreed to build a new service with it. Warner Music settled with Suno a month later and announced a [partnership](https://techcrunch.com/2025/11/25/warner-music-signs-deal-with-ai-music-startup-suno-settles-lawsuit/). Prompt-to-song is being absorbed into the old chain as a catalogue business.

That settles a legal question. It does not settle a musical one.

A prompt steers only approximately, because words fall short of what a person hears: "a warmer bass" describes a thousand basses, and people usually find the one they meant by hearing it. The makers know it. In September 2025 Suno launched [Suno Studio](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/suno-introduces-suno-studio-a-generative-audio-workstation-built-for-all-creatives-from-seasoned-pros-to-aspiring-artists-302567486.html), which it calls a generative audio workstation, with a multitrack timeline and stems; it had bought a browser DAW, WavTool, and called the result "the next chapter for the evolution of the DAW." Its [second version](https://routenote.com/radar/suno-studio-2-0-launches-with-midi-support-ai-chat-and-advanced-stem-separation/) added MIDI editing and an assistant you can ask to do production tasks in conversation. From the other side, Apple added AI [session players and a stem splitter](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2024/05/logic-pro-takes-music-making-to-the-next-level-with-new-ai-features/) to Logic Pro in 2024. The generator is growing a workstation, and the workstation is growing generators. Each is approaching the same missing thing from its own side, control over the sound, and each is an early signal of the DAW of the future.

None of them has closed the gap. In the generators, the sound itself is still asked for in words, and no sentence says exactly what a sound is.

**Generation becomes part of production only when it has an editable target to generate into**: a project that the person and the agent can both open, hear and change, so that generated sound arrives as material rather than as a sealed object, and you can point at what you mean instead of describing it. Recordings remain precious, and sampling culture is built on them. But a recording is something you listen to. A project is something you continue. Programming learned that distinction first.

## Programming went first

Software development has already been through the transition music is entering. For decades, code was written in an editor, built by a compiler and explained by a manual, three tools learned separately before anything worked. Now a developer states an objective; an assistant changes the files directly and runs the tests; version control records every change with its author; the developer keeps or reverts, and asks again. Learning first and building after has given way to a loop: ask, look, correct, continue. Agents now write much of the code, and on the hardest contest problems they beat almost every human. One of the largest software companies reportedly says AI now generates [about three-quarters of its new code](https://www.techspot.com/news/112152-google-ai-now-generates-75-new-code-up.html), up from a quarter in 2024. In 2025 an AI system [outscored all but five](https://the-decoder.com/openais-ai-system-wins-a-gold-medal-level-score-at-the-international-olympiad-in-informatics-2025/) of the 330 students at the International Olympiad in Informatics, and at the world finals of the main university programming contest an AI system [solved all twelve problems](https://the-decoder.com/openai-outperforms-humans-and-google-at-the-worlds-top-collegiate-programming-contest/); the best human team solved eleven.

What made this work was not the chat window. It was four quieter things: **a shared editable object** (the repository), **the same commands** for the person and the agent (the agent edits files like any other editor), **senses** (compilers, tests and logs that tell the agent what actually happened), and **memory** (version control, so nothing is lost and every change has a name). "Cursor for music", after the AI code editor, is shorthand for the same four things in a song. *Bring the bass back four bars later and leave the vocal alone*: the agent makes that change in the song itself, and the person hears it in context and keeps or reverts it.

It did not work at once. In October 2023, the best model tested [resolved 1.96%](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06770) of a set of real software issues; within about two years, leading agents were resolving [most of a verified set](https://www.swebench.com/) of them. Music shares a good part of what made that possible: structure, pattern and logic, held in an editable project. It also has a dimension code does not have: sound, and an ear to judge it. The shared part is already within reach. In my own work, agents build whole songs and remixes through an instrument's commands; the songs are complete and they play, and a person still has to hear what is wrong and say so. On an eleven-level ladder I use to judge agent-made songs, from a song's basic shape up to one I would pick over the best out there, the best agents so far reach the third or fourth level, and not yet reliably. What separates music from code is not a number of years. It is the new dimension, and it opens as music gets what code had: an editable medium, senses and memory.

My bet is that the general agents that mastered code will also be the producers. Anything with pattern and logic in it is open to them. They will reach sound through generators, synthesis written as code and neural sound models alike, and they will build their own tools to hear what works and what does not, find the patterns and use those tools in production, the way they already write tests for their own code.

The analogy bends at one point, and it is the most important point in this essay. Much of what passes for taste is craft. Some ways of building a song or a sound make it work, others make it dull or irritating, and listeners agree on a good part of that. You may dislike a style and still hear that a track in it is well made, while the people who love that style would turn away from the same track badly produced.

Craft has patterns, and some of them can be measured: in [most studies that have tested it](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0305735617697507), liking tends to rise with complexity up to a point and then fall, so that too simple is dull and too much is irritating, and much of what makes a sound unpleasant lies [between about 2,000 and 5,000 hertz](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/jneuro/32/41/14184.full.pdf), unless, as in hard techno, harshness is the point. That part agents can learn, the way they learned code, and the tools they build will check it.

What remains is taste in the narrow sense: which of many well-made things is yours, and it [moves](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0281057) with everything you have heard and with the moment. Whether harshness is the point depends on intent and mood: people [prefer arousing music](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/12573161_Musical_Preferences_during_and_after_Relaxation_and_Exercise) while exercising and calmer music while relaxing, and the hard techno that lifts a room at three in the morning can be merely irritating on a quiet afternoon. A measurement can find the harshness; only the listener knows whether it is wanted now. **Tests can check much of the craft. Only you can check your taste.** In music, the last test is the ear of the person the music is for. The new actions below rest on that.

## New actions for old wants

The wants have always been there. What new technology changes is the actions available for reaching them, and every new action faces the same test: does it get you to what you want cheaper and faster? That is what anyone wants from a tool, from a customer waiting for an Amazon parcel to a producer who wants to hear a sound the moment it is imagined. Agents, and instruments that measure, open six new actions.

### Ask for what you mean, at any depth

People think about music in musical terms: hear the whole, attend to a part, change a sound, hear the result in context. Now they can ask at whatever depth the intention lives ("let the chorus arrive later" is arrangement; "a warmer bass" is sound), as far as the material honestly supports; a recording does not automatically yield clean parts, and a good instrument says so rather than pretending. The bass that should wait a little longer before it returns becomes one request and a few seconds of listening, not an evening.

It is played the way people learn: ask, hear, adjust, continue. The first useful action needs no knowledge of the whole tool: a beginner may stop after one change, while an expert may need one note in one bar, and the same work serves both. **A low floor, never a low ceiling.**

### Work on one song with an agent

In the next DAW, an agent is not a separate app that sends back a file. It edits the song directly, like any other editor, within the access the person grants, whether that is one change or a whole task such as a kit for a fast, hard track. Nothing has to be exported, imported or explained twice: the change is in the song the moment it is made. Every operation the agent performs is one the person could perform by hand, so the agent's work can also teach.

There is no private project that only the agent can see, and no change that exists in the conversation but not in the music. Close the conversation, and every change and every control stays where it was.

### The instrument measures, you listen

An assistant that cannot perceive the music will invent it: a confident description that says nothing true, a key guessed from a filename, a layer that fights the kick. Agents will hear one day. Models that listen to music are improving quickly, though their makers still [describe music](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.10289) as hard for them to understand. But hearing is not feeling. A model can collect data on how people respond to music and learn a good deal about what works on them, yet music is felt with the body as well as the ear. A kick that hits your chest at a festival does not feel like the same kick played quietly at home: the sound pressing on your body, and your body falling into time with the beat, are part of what the music does. A model takes in the waveform as numbers. Music is a tool for emotional regulation, and that regulation happens in the body of the person listening, so however good the models become, that part stays human.

Until agents hear well, the work divides: the person listens, and the instrument measures and does the sound engineering that measurement can settle, such as levels, the balance between parts, the low end, clipping and loudness. Engineering that once took a specialist hours can be done in seconds, and the listening stays with you.

That means musical evidence measured by the instrument itself, not imagined by a model: the key as a ranked list of candidates rather than one guessed label, the beat grid with its confidence, energy and brightness over time, and whether a new part would fill a gap or clash with what is there. When the evidence is ambiguous, the agent should receive the ambiguity. The evidence keeps its value after agents can hear, just as producers with good ears still use meters: two assistants on two different days should read the same facts about the same audio. **The agent's judgment may vary; the evidence must not.**

### Sounds that arrive finished and stay editable

A producer who wants an industrial bass for a hard techno track wants that bass, finished. Samples will keep that role. What changes is what stands behind the sample: when a sound carries its editable source, it can be re-keyed, re-timed or re-shaped when the song's needs change, instead of being searched for again. **The sample, the preset and the lesson become one object**: a finished sound, the settings that make it, and an explanation an agent can give. A sound that carries its settings is, to an agent, much like code: something it can read, change and explain.

### Sounds no one has heard yet

Every new generator has widened the range of sounds, and new styles grew around the new sounds. The [TR-808](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roland_TR-808), sold from 1980, had a bass drum with a long decay that producers stretched and tuned into the bass lines of hip hop and later trap. The supersaw of Roland's [JP-8000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roland_JP-8000), from 1996, sounded like seven sawtooth waves detuned against each other; it defined trance and then spread through dance music into the pop charts. By the late 2000s dubstep had made the [wobble bass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dubstep), a synthesizer's filter swept by a slow oscillator, its signature. In much of today's techno the kick [does the work of the bass line](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/tutorials/processing-berghain-kicks-with-multiband-distortion/) as well as the beat, and its weight is felt as much as heard. In a study published in 2022, researchers at a live electronic music concert switched on and off speakers that played only frequencies between 8 and 37 hertz, which listeners in a separate test could not detect; the audience [moved 11.8% more](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2022.09.035) while they were on. The want is old: a sound that moves you more than the last one did.

The range will keep widening. My bet is that the next new sounds will be designed first in sound generators and then in neural sound engines, and that coding agents will master sound design first, in the generators, because there a sound is a set of settings: an agent can change them, render the sound, measure it and try again far faster than anyone can turn knobs, and bring back only the results worth hearing.

That work will leave behind the data neural sound engines need: sounds designed for music, each paired with the settings that make it and with the verdict of the person who kept it or threw it away. Pairs alone are cheap. In 2021 researchers released [a billion synthesizer sounds](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.12922), each paired with the settings that made it; but the settings were drawn at random, for no song and no ear. Neural engines can already reach past any one generator: in 2017 Google's [NSynth](https://magenta.tensorflow.org/nsynth), trained on about 300,000 notes from about 1,000 instruments, blended a flute and an organ into sounds that would be hard or impossible to make with a synthesizer tuned by hand. Trained on what agents design and people keep, the next engines can open dimensions in the space of sounds that no generator's controls reach. **The generators teach the agents; what the agents make teaches the neural engines.** Which of the new sounds become music is decided, as always, by the people who feel them.

### Every version kept

Programmers take version control for granted; musicians mostly do without it. In time the DAW of the future will give music the same memory: every change recorded with its maker, person, collaborator or agent, and any kept version heard, compared with any other and returned to, with nothing lost by going back. That memory comes after the sound. First an instrument whose sounds people want to keep their music in; then, on it, a memory they can rely on.

Memory makes experimentation safe, because the ending you had is never at risk while an agent tries three others, and it makes music continuable. Beyond it, I am betting on a hub, music's version of shared code: songs published in full editable form, every part of the production and every sound open to change, so that people continue and improve each other's music the way programmers continue each other's code. Someone hears a song, enters the drum part, tries a different rhythm and keeps a descendant. The original stays intact, and its maker can hear the answer and continue from it in turn. **A work becomes an invitation to a musical answer, and a good answer becomes another beginning.** Such a hub would also give music what public code gave programming: a body of editable work that people, and agents, can learn from. It is a bet, and sharing stays an act the person chooses. So does the last word.

## The verdict stays with the ear

Who is the author of a song made with an agent? The person who directs the work, who writes the brief, guides each pass and decides what stays. The record keeps what an agent or a generator contributed distinguishable, so the history tells the truth about how the music was made. **Directing an agent is a form of expression, not the absence of one.** Music is music, whether made by hand, with an agent or in any tool; what matters is access to its building blocks, so that it can be heard, changed and built on.

What must never move is the verdict. Evidence is what the instrument measures; the verdict is what the ear decides, and no reading can stand in for it. A loudness, a pitch and a brightness describe a sound; whether it is a kick you would use is your answer, given after listening. This does not rest on machines being deaf. When agents can hear, they will judge their own attempts better and set more of them aside. The verdict stays with you because the music is for you: only you feel what it does, and only you can say whether it is what you meant. An agent may advise; it may not put its choice in place of yours.

That is the whole thesis. Here is what would break it.

## What could prove this wrong

It is a thesis, not a prophecy, and each objection to it can be tested.

**Most people may simply want to listen.** If people offered an easy way to change a song mostly go on listening, the demand for shaping is a minority one, and the instrument must be honest about that.

**Participation can be attractive and still lose.** Player-piano sales in America [peaked in 1924](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Player_piano); within a few years the electrical phonograph and radio had swept them aside, and the crash of 1929 ended their prominence. Many buyers seem to have valued the outcome more than the act of steering. The question for any new instrument is whether people return for the act itself, or only because it is, for now, the easiest way to a result.

**The new dimension may not yield.** Coding agents learned from millions of public repositories and check their own work with tests. Music's new dimension has neither at that scale: people publish recordings, not projects, and the tests for craft are still to be built. If sound and craft do not yield to pattern and logic the way code did, general agents will remain skilled assistants rather than producers.

**The new sounds may not come from search.** Sounds that started styles have often come from people using a tool against its design: the TB-303 found its sound three years after it was discontinued. Neural engines trained on what generators make may learn to blend the sounds we know without finding ones anyone wants. Then agents will refine the range of sounds rather than widen it.

**The incumbents can absorb it.** Free, integrated studios already reach enormous numbers of people, and the established DAWs are already bringing assistants inside. A new instrument must be clearly better at something people do often.

**Listening may cost more than the agent saves.** An agent that works while you are away can leave you with more changes than you can hear. If people stop listening to what agents change, the agents' reach must narrow.

**Recorded character may win.** A recording carries performance, room and history that synthesis may never reach. Offered an editable and a frozen version of the same part, producers may choose the frozen one. Then recordings must stand beside editable sounds as first-class material.

**Consent and displacement are unresolved.** New instruments have always met organized objection from the players they displaced; synthesizers did too. Assistance that can make whole songs raises the question again, together with the consent of the musicians whose recordings trained the models. In June 2026 the American Federation of Musicians [sued](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/musicians-union-sues-umg-and-warner-music-alleging-member-recordings-were-licensed-to-suno-and-udio-without-compensation-or-credit/) Universal and Warner, alleging that recordings made by its members were passed to Suno and Udio without compensation or credit. These questions do not have engineering answers.

## The order of change

Programming shows the order in which this happens, because it has just been through it:

| Step | Programming | Music |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chat and copy | From late 2022, people asked [ChatGPT](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt/) for code and pasted the answers into their editors | People ask a chatbot about a sound or a mix and carry the answer into the DAW by hand |
| Chat in the tool | From 2023, [Cursor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursor_(code_editor)), built on VS Code, brought the chat into the editor, where it could read the project and change the code | Assistants inside the workstation, such as Logic Pro's session players |
| The agent does the work | In 2025, [Claude Code](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-3-7-sonnet) and [Codex](https://openai.com/index/introducing-codex/) began reading whole projects, editing the files, running the tests and fixing what failed | The agent works on the song itself through the instrument's actions, and the editable project becomes what people and agents share |
| You judge the result | In 2026, more and more code is written without anyone reading it line by line; the developer checks what it does and guides the next change | You listen to how the music sounds and give your feedback, and the agent produces |

Most music is still between the first two steps; in my own work, the third has begun. The last step is the one this essay is about: you will guide music by how it sounds, the way developers now guide code by what it does, while the agent does the writing.

The change will also arrive with a new generation. People who start making music with an interactive instrument, one that guides them toward what they want to hear, will not want to work with tools that make them learn the operation first, just as few producers who started on a laptop ever went looking for a tape machine.

**The classical studio idea will not die the day an alternative becomes better at everything. It starts to die the day an alternative becomes better at the thing people do most: changing a song and coming back to it.**

The DAW of the past carried the classical studio idea: you had to learn it before it would serve you. The DAW of the future is played interactively: it meets you at your intention, lets an agent carry the work you do not want to carry, keeps every choice you might want back, and leaves the last word to your ear.

The classical studio idea is still standing. But the first versions of its successor are already on the workbench.
