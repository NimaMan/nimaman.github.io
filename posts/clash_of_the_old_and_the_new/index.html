The Clash

Clash of the Old and the New

Nov 02, 2026 Nima Manaf, PhD 23 min read

Around the age of thirteen, the brain begins to respond more to unfamiliar voices than to a mother's, part of what prepares the young to leave the nest and build lives of their own. The same turn shows from the individual to the state. Every better, cheaper or faster technology reaches people as an unfamiliar voice: the young take it up first, the old holds on with whatever power it has, a guild, a trade or a state, and reality shifts the balance. Each time a capability got cheaper, whoever held the expensive version lost its power: the empire that rose on the cannon took up the printing press three centuries late and fell in the age of the factory, and today drones costing hundreds of dollars meet machines costing millions.

Play a child of ten a recording of their mother saying a nonsense word, less than a second long, and the child will almost always know whose voice it is. In one study, children aged seven to twelve recognized their mother from such clips more than 97 percent of the time, and her voice drove the brain’s regions for voices, emotion, reward and faces harder than the voices of women they did not know. In teenagers the answer changes. A 2022 study of 46 children and teenagers aged 7 to 16 measured two regions that weigh reward and social value, the nucleus accumbens and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex. In the children they responded more to the mother’s voice; from about thirteen and a half, they responded more to unfamiliar voices. The teenagers’ brains still answered their mothers. What grew was the pull of voices they had never heard.

The researchers read the change this way: “a hallmark of adolescence is a shift in orientation toward nonfamilial social targets, an adaptive process that prepares adolescents for their independence.” It is not dislike. It is the brain preparing the young to leave the nest and build a life of their own, among people their parents do not know.

The turn is far older than our species. Across mammals, a review of the adolescent brain found that “age-related increases in social behavior and risk-taking/novelty-seeking, are common among adolescents of diverse mammalian species.” Many young mammals then leave the group they were born into: in most chimpanzee communities, females leave at about 11 to 13, around their first menstruation, and baboon males leave their troop at a median age of 8½, as they near adult size. In people, a study of 5,404 young people in eleven countries, from China, India and Kenya to Sweden and the United States, found that the appetite for new and intense experience rises through adolescence and peaks at 19.

The same turn can be seen in action at every level, from the individual to the society: in a teenager’s brain, in animals leaving home, in a generation’s taste, in sciences that move on when their leaders die, in trades, and in nations that collapse because they resist the new. The clash of the old and the new begins in human nature. A better, cheaper or faster way of doing something reaches people as an unfamiliar voice. The young turn toward it first. The old holds on to what it knows, sometimes with the power of a guild, an industry or a state. Then reality decides, and sooner or later the old gives way. Sometimes a state falls with it.

The old holds on

The other side of the clash is built in too. The music people hear in their early teens stays with them: in a study of 470 people aged 18 to 82, memories of popular songs and familiarity with them peaked for music from the listener’s adolescence, around the age of 14. Adults also tend to believe the young have got worse. In five studies of 3,458 American adults, people judged today’s children to have declined in exactly the traits where they themselves excelled, and older adults were more likely to see the young as less respectful. The authors traced the belief to two habits of mind: noticing the shortcomings of others where one excels, and remembering one’s own youth with one’s present qualities.

People also weigh what they might lose more heavily than what they might gain. Across 607 estimates, a loss weighed on average about twice as much as a gain of the same size. And they stay with whatever is already in place: in one comparison of European countries, effective consent to organ donation ran from about 4 to 28 percent where people had to opt in, and from 86 to almost 100 percent where they had to opt out. Those who built the old world feel the new as a loss, and the old world is the default.

So the clash is not between good people and bad ones. The young are built to reach for the unfamiliar, and the old to keep what it knows and has made. Each acts on its nature, and the meeting of the two is the clash.

One funeral at a time

Science, which prides itself on changing its mind when the evidence changes, shows how the turn happens in practice. Economists followed the fields of 452 eminent life scientists who died prematurely. After each death, the star’s collaborators published less in the field, while articles by outsiders rose by 8.6 percent on average; the newcomers drew on different work, and their articles were disproportionately likely to be highly cited. The field moved when its leader was gone.

A whole science can stall the same way, and in fundamental physics little has moved since the decades of the bomb. Quantum mechanics was worked out from 1925, nuclear fission was discovered in 1938, the first atomic bombs exploded in 1945 and the first hydrogen bomb in 1952, and the Standard Model of particle physics was complete by the mid-1970s. The great discoveries since were predictions of that era or earlier: the top quark, predicted in 1973, was found in 1995; the Higgs boson, predicted in 1964, in 2012; gravitational waves, predicted in 1916, in 2015. The latest advances follow the same line. In December 2022 the National Ignition Facility reached fusion ignition, releasing 3.15 megajoules from the 2.05 delivered by lasers that drew about 300 from the grid. In June 2025 the final measurement of the muon’s magnetism, precise to 127 parts per billion, came out close to the latest Standard Model prediction. The 2025 Nobel Prize in Physics went to experiments from 1984 and 1985. The Large Hadron Collider, colliding protons since 2010, found the Higgs boson and, by CERN’s 2025 review, no new particle beyond the Standard Model, and no experiment has confirmed string theory, worked on since the 1970s. The prizes show the lag: since 1985, 60 percent of the Nobel Prizes in physics have been awarded more than twenty years after the discovery, against 11 percent before 1940. Keeping chip density doubling on schedule has taken more than 18 times the researchers it took in the early 1970s.

The new does not come in by argument. It comes in when the old steps aside, carried by people who had not been part of it, and where the old frame holds, a whole field can wait half a century for its next turn. Technology runs on the same turn, with one more force behind it.

Better, cheaper, faster

That force is cost. When a better, cheaper or faster way to reach the same want appears, people take it, as I argued in The Selection Pressure: the want is the constant, and the means keep dying. Nobody has to be persuaded. The want takes the shorter road, and the young take it first. In 2025, 58 percent of American adults under 30 had used ChatGPT, against 10 percent of those over 65. Using the new is not the same as admiring it: a year later, adults under 30 were also the most likely to expect AI to harm society, 48 percent against 35 percent of those over 65. The young use what works, and their use, not their opinion, is what moves the old.

Where the old can only argue, it fades. The natural ice trade employed an estimated 90,000 people at its peak; its harvesters formed an association, and companies played on the false belief that natural ice melted more slowly than ice made by machine. In the 1930s the share of American homes with a refrigerator rose from 8 to 44 percent, by one count, and the trade collapsed. American farms held 26.7 million horses and mules in 1918. The Horse Association of America, formed by the horse trades in 1919, declared that “A mule is the only fool proof tractor ever built” and staged hundreds of demonstrations of big teams; by 1960 farms had more than 4.5 million tractors and fewer than 3 million draft animals.

The powerful old resists, and reality kicks in

Where the old holds power, it uses it, and it can hold the new back for generations. In 1551 Parliament passed “An Act for putting down of Gig Mills”, protecting the croppers who raised the nap on woollen cloth by hand. The statute stood for 258 years, until clothiers campaigned against it and Parliament repealed it in 1809 with dozens of other protective laws. The croppers turned to breaking machines. From March 1811 the Luddites wrecked frames across the Midlands and the north, Parliament made frame-breaking a capital felony in 1812, and the government sent 12,000 troops. By one estimate, shearing frames rose from 100 in 1806 to 1,462 in 1817, when more than a third of the remaining croppers were out of work.

The roads went the same way. In 1831 a Commons committee found tolls set “to an amount which would utterly prohibit the introduction of Steam Carriages”, out of “a determination on the part of the Trustees, to obstruct, as much as possible, the use of Steam”. The Locomotives Act 1865 limited road engines to 4 mph in the country and 2 in towns and required a man to walk 60 yards ahead of each with a red flag. The flag went in 1878, but the walker stayed in law until 1896, when the new motor industry lobbied for light vehicles to be freed.

In the United States the dairy industry fought margarine with the law for most of a century. A federal act of 1886 taxed it and licensed its makers, six states banned it outright, and by 1902, 32 states restricted its colour; three required it to be dyed pink until the Supreme Court struck those laws down. Price decided anyway. In the Depression and the wartime butter shortages margarine began to take butter’s place, the federal tax ended in 1951, and the last state colour law, Wisconsin’s, in 1967.

Trading powers fought the same way. The Hanseatic League, the league of merchant towns that dominated the trade of the Baltic, fought a privateering war from 1438 to 1441 to keep the ships of Holland out, and lost. From about 1595 the Dutch built the fluit, a cargo ship that could be crewed “by half as many men as a traditional vessel, whilst construction costs were reduced by almost 50 per cent”. The League held its last general assembly in 1669, and in 1670 the Dutch merchant marine totalled 568,000 tons, “about half the European total”.

Unions fight with contracts and strikes. American theatres employed about 22,000 musicians in their pits in 1926; sound film arrived in 1927, and within two years, by the union’s own later account, most of them were out of work. From August 1942 the American Federation of Musicians barred its members from making records. The ban held for two years and ended with the record companies paying into a union fund. The pit orchestras never came back, and recorded music became the ordinary way to hear music.

Licences hold for a while too. A New York taxi medallion, the licence for a yellow cab, reportedly peaked at over $1 million around 2013, as ride-hailing apps let drivers without one take fares. Taxi drivers blocked roads in major European cities in 2014, France banned the unlicensed service, and New York capped new ride-hail licences in 2018. Medallions still fell below $200,000, and in 2022 the yellow cabs joined the rival’s app.

In each case the old’s power bought time: 258 years for the croppers’ statute, 31 for the man with the flag, most of a century for butter, more than two centuries for the Hanse. In none did it stop the new. What decided was not argument but price, capability and the people who used the new: the clothiers who bought the frames, the buyers of cars and of margarine, the Dutch shipowners, the cinemagoers and listeners, the riders booking a car on a phone.

When the old is the state

Between states the same law sets the balance of power. Each time a military capability became cheaper or faster, whoever held the expensive version lost its leverage, and the equations that move the balance are plain ones: what a capability costs for the effect it delivers, what it costs the other side to stop it, and how many of it can be made. When the old holds the state, its resistance can cost the state itself.

The clearest case is an empire that rose on the new. The Ottoman Janissaries were perhaps the first infantry force in the world to be equipped mainly with firearms, in the first half of the 1400s. In 1452 a Hungarian cannon founder offered his services to the Byzantine state, by then a city of fewer than 50,000 people, which could not pay him; the Ottomans could. The land walls of Constantinople, built in 413 and 447, had stood for a thousand years without being taken by assault. His great gun, about eight metres long, threw stone balls of several hundred kilograms and took 60 oxen to haul. On 6 April 1453 an Ottoman army of 50,000 to 80,000 began the siege against 7,000 to 8,000 defenders, who repaired the walls every night; on 29 May the city fell, and the last Byzantine states followed by 1461. At Chaldiran in 1514 the Ottomans brought 100 to 200 cannon and thousands of musketeers against a Safavid army that had no artillery, and the Safavid cavalry, attacking the wings to avoid the guns, suffered disastrous losses. The Mamluk Sultanate, whose army was built on its mamluk cavalry, had set up a gun foundry and a musketeer corps in 1507, recruited from outside that cavalry; at Marj Dabiq in August 1516 its sultan was killed, at Ridaniya in January 1517 the guns it had gathered at the last minute failed, and by April it was annexed. The Ottomans were the new, and the old fell before them.

Then the new became the old, and the printing press shows it most plainly. Printing with movable type began in Mainz in the middle of the 1400s and spread quickly: Western Europe printed about 12.6 million books before 1500, 217 million in the 1500s and close to a billion in the 1700s, and between 1500 and 1600 European cities where presses were set up in the 1400s grew 60 percent faster than otherwise similar cities. In the Ottoman Empire, Jewish, Armenian and Greek presses worked from 1493, but the first press for books in Ottoman Turkish opened only in 1727. By 1743 it had issued 17 works in 23 volumes, in runs of 500 to 1,000 copies, and then it stopped. Learning travels in books, and invention follows learning by several decades. The empire took up the press close to three centuries late, its learning and its invention slowed, and the inventions of the next age, from the steam engine to the power loom, came from elsewhere.

The army went the same way. From the late 1500s the sons of Janissaries were admitted to the corps, a practice once forbidden, and the corps became a hereditary, privileged body. In the 1790s the sultan raised a new army on European lines, about 23,000 men by 1806. The Janissaries refused to serve beside it, and in May 1807 their revolt, backed by a religious ruling, deposed the reforming sultan, who was killed the next year. The reform waited until 15 June 1826, when the new artillery set the Janissaries’ barracks aflame, killing about 4,000 of them and ending a corps 463 years old.

At sea the same turn came through fire, steam and the explosive shell. In July 1770 a Russian squadron burned almost the whole Ottoman fleet in the bay of Chesma; at least 11,000 Ottoman sailors died, against several hundred Russians. At Navarino on 20 October 1827, British, French and Russian ships destroyed the Ottoman-Egyptian fleet without losing one of their own: “Of the entire Ottoman-Egyptian armada of 78 vessels, just eight remained seaworthy.” At Sinop on 30 November 1853, a Russian squadron firing explosive shells destroyed 15 Ottoman vessels in an afternoon; one steamer escaped, about 3,000 Ottoman sailors were killed, and the Russians lost 37.

On land the Russian Empire advanced, treaty by treaty, against the Ottomans, the Qajar Empire and the khanates of the Turkic world. At Küçük Kaynarca in 1774 the Ottomans gave up the Crimean Khanate, declared “free and independent”, and Russia annexed it in 1783. Jassy in 1792 moved the border to the Dniester, Bucharest in 1812 gave Russia Bessarabia, Adrianople in 1829 the mouths of the Danube and the forts of the eastern Black Sea coast, and the Treaty of Berlin in 1878 Kars, Ardahan and Batumi, while Romania, Serbia and Montenegro became independent and Bulgaria was split in three. The Qajar Empire, whose army a Russian force of about 2,000 men with six guns routed at Aslanduz in 1812, ceded the khanates of Karabakh, Ganja, Shaki, Shirvan, Derbent, Quba and Baku at Gulistan in 1813, and those of Erivan and Nakhichevan at Turkmenchay in 1828, where the border was set on the Aras river. The Aras still divides the Azerbaijani Turks: by most estimates more of them live to its south than in the Republic of Azerbaijan to its north. In the North Caucasus, a war that ran from 1817 to 1864 ended with up to half a million Circassians fleeing to the Ottoman Empire. In Central Asia a Russian force of under 2,000 men with 12 guns stormed Tashkent in 1865 for 25 dead. Samarkand fell in 1868 and the Emirate of Bukhara was bound to follow Russian direction; the Khanate of Khiva fell in 1873, and the Khanate of Kokand was annexed in 1876. At Geok Tepe in January 1881 about 7,000 Russian troops with some 70 guns and rockets took a fortress held by 20,000 to 25,000 Turkmen, about 8,000 of them with firearms and none with artillery, and their commander reported: “6,500 bodies were buried inside it. During the pursuit 8,000 were killed.” To supply the march, the army had begun a railway across the desert, because it could not find enough camels.

By then the age of the factory had come, and the empire met it as the old. A trade treaty with Britain in 1838 cut its duties to 3 percent and opened its markets to factory goods it did not make, and machine production stayed a small part of its output. On 30 October 1875 the government defaulted on its foreign loans, and from 1881 a Public Debt Administration run by its European creditors collected taxes and handed them to the creditors: “At its peak it had 9,000 employees, more than the empire’s finance ministry.” Greece had become independent in 1830; the empire lost the war of 1877–78, most of its European lands in 1912–13 and, after the armistice of Mudros on 30 October 1918, its Arab provinces. The Treaty of Sèvres of 1920, never ratified, would have partitioned what remained. The sultanate was abolished on 1 November 1922, and the Treaty of Lausanne of July 1923 drew the borders of the republic proclaimed that October. The empire that rose because it had the cannon fell in the age of the factory.

China met the same age from the throne. In 1793 a British embassy reached the Qing court asking for trade and a resident envoy, and the imperial reply said: “As your Ambassador can see for himself, we possess all things. I set no value on objects strange or ingenious, and have no use for your country’s manufactures.” In the First Opium War the British brought steam. On 7 January 1841, at the second battle of Chuenpi, British forces that included the East India Company’s iron steamer destroyed 11 war junks; 277 Chinese were killed and 467 wounded, against 38 British wounded. The Treaty of Nanking of August 1842 ceded Hong Kong, opened five ports and imposed 21 million silver dollars.

The court then bought the machines and kept its institutions. Under the Self-Strengthening Movement of about 1861 to 1895, provincial arsenals made rifles “not only more costly than, but also far inferior to, the imported Remington arms”, and ships “at least twice as costly as comparable vessels available for purchase in Britain”. In 1894 Japan, which had changed its institutions, went to war with China; at Shimonoseki in April 1895 China ceded Taiwan and paid 200 million taels. The New Army raised after that defeat rose on 10 October 1911, and on 12 February 1912 the dynasty abdicated.

The Soviet Union met the computer the same way. In March 1985 a CIA assessment put the Soviet Union behind the United States in every field of general-purpose computing, by at least three years and in some fields by more than ten. A national computer network had been proposed in 1962; its funding was refused in 1970 amid quarrels between ministries. The Union was dissolved on 26 December 1991.

The nation that let its old order go

Japan met the same new and chose the other way. On 8 July 1853 four American warships, two of them steamers, anchored in Edo Bay, and a treaty followed the next March. Within fifteen years the shogunate was gone. The restoration of January 1868 was carried out by young samurai of the Satsuma and Chōshū domains, many of them of humble birth; its core leaders were between 26 and 39 years old, with a median age of about 30. They then took apart their own class. The domains were abolished in 1871, the first railway opened in 1872, conscription came in 1873, and in 1876 swords were banned in public and the samurai’s stipends, which had cost a quarter to a third of all government spending, were converted by force into ¥173.9 million of bonds.

The old fought back once. In 1877 the Satsuma Rebellion, led by one of the restoration’s own leaders, was put down by a conscript army that used 322,000 rounds of ammunition and 1,000 artillery shells a day; the rebels had rifles and guns too, and numbers and supply decided. Eighteen years later Japan defeated Qing China, and in 1905 it defeated Russia.

Japan kept its nation by letting its old order go, and the old order’s own young let it go. The Qing court kept its old order and lost the dynasty. The Ottoman state kept its Janissaries a generation too long and met the factory with its workshops, and lost the empire.

The cost of force falls again

The Second World War left the balance of force with the side that made the most machines: by 1945, by one account, “the United States was manufacturing more than half of the produced goods in the world”. The bomb then froze the top of the balance. No two great powers have gone to war with each other since the Korean War ended in 1953, and the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki remain the only uses of nuclear weapons in war. Below the bomb, military power came to rest on the most expensive machines ever built. A Ford-class aircraft carrier costs about $13 billion, an F-35A fighter about $90 million, and the missiles that defend a fleet $2 million to $4 million a shot.

In the last ten years the cost of precision fell by orders of magnitude, and the equations moved with it. In the 44 days of the 2020 war over Nagorno-Karabakh, Azerbaijan’s Turkish and Israeli drones and loitering munitions led an offensive in which Armenian forces lost more than 200 tanks and dozens of air-defence systems, by a count of losses confirmed in photographs and video. In Ukraine a first-person-view drone costs about $300 to $500. Ukraine made about 2.2 million drones in 2024 and more than 4 million in 2025, and by early 2025 small drones accounted for “60–70% of damaged and destroyed Russian systems”. With almost no navy, Ukraine used sea drones costing about $250,000 to sink warships worth tens of millions, and from October 2023 Russia moved most of its Black Sea Fleet out of Sevastopol. On 1 June 2025, 117 small drones launched from trucks near five airbases struck strategic bombers deep inside Russia, and satellite photos showed seven destroyed bombers at a single base in eastern Siberia.

The equations are plain. In the Red Sea, American warships fired “nearly $2 million missiles to defeat drones that cost as little as $2,000”, a ratio of a thousand to one; in the 15 months to January 2025 the Navy fired about 120 SM-2 and 80 SM-6 missiles. A Shahed drone is reckoned to cost tens of thousands of dollars, and the interceptor sent to stop it can cost a hundred times as much. A sea drone of $250,000 against a warship of $65 million is a ratio of one to more than two hundred. The leverage shifts to whoever can make the cheap thing in quantity, and to the costs that have not yet fallen: in the same study of the front, “between 60 and 80% of Ukrainian FPVs fail to reach their target”, so jamming, production lines, chips and trained operators become the scarce costs.

The largest military is changing its own rules. In August 2023 the US Department of Defense set out to field “attritable autonomous systems at scale of multiple thousands, in multiple domains, within the next 18-to-24 months”. In July 2025, as that deadline passed, the Defense Secretary ordered that small drones be treated as “consumable commodities, not durable property”, and in December the department asked industry for about 340,000 small drones over two years, at target prices falling from $5,000 to $2,300 each.

The order repeats at every scale. The young and the outsiders carry the new: the newcomers to a dead scientist’s field, the clothiers, the riders, the conscripts, the young samurai. The old resists with whatever power it holds: claims and demonstrations where it can only argue, statutes and troops where it holds Parliament, a revolt where it holds the barracks, a refusal where it holds the throne. The resistance buys years, sometimes centuries, and does not stop the new; price, capability and the people who use the new shift the balance. Between states the equations are cost per effect, cost exchange and production scale, and each time they have turned toward the cheaper capability made in quantity. A trade that resists loses its trade. A state that resists can lose itself, unless, as in Japan, the old order’s own young take it apart first. And the new, once it holds the field, becomes the next old: the Janissaries were the firearms of the 1400s and the obstacle of 1807.

The new that is arriving now

General AI agents are this decade’s unfamiliar voice, and they are arriving in many fields at once: writing, translation, images, music and code. The clash has begun in each. The Writers Guild of America struck from May to September 2023, and under its new contract “AI can’t write or rewrite literary material”; actors struck over pay and digital replicas; record labels sued the song generators and then licensed them. In June 2026 the American Federation of Musicians, the union that fought sound film and records, sued two record companies for licensing its members’ recordings to those generators without paying them.

Where the old holds a licence, it holds longest. By 2025, by one count, American regulators had cleared more than 700 radiology models, yet malpractice policies often covered only “interpretations reviewed and authenticated by a licensed physician”, and radiology programs offered a record number of training places. The pattern does not say when the new arrives in each field. It says what follows: the young take it up first, the old resists with the power it holds, and the resistance buys time rather than survival. Where a state takes the old’s side against the new, the cost may again fall on the state.

What could prove this wrong

Two things would break the pattern. The first is a powerful old that stops a better, cheaper or faster technology for good, by law or by force, while the want it served is still there; none of the cases above did. The second is a generation that turns from using the new to refusing it. The young are the heaviest users of AI and the most doubtful of it; if their doubt outweighs their use, the new will wait for the generation after them.

In 1793 the Qing court had no use for foreign manufactures, and in 1912 the dynasty abdicated. In 1807 the Janissaries overthrew the sultan who built a new army, and in 1922 the sultanate was abolished. In 1868 young samurai took Japan’s government apart, and their own class with it, and in 1905 Japan defeated Russia. The old resisted in all three. The difference was whether the young were allowed to leave the nest.
