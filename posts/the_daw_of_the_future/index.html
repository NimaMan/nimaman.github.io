Music

The DAW of the Future

Oct 29, 2026 Nima Manaf, PhD 19 min read

For more than thirty years the digital audio workstation has been a studio you must learn before it will serve you. Now that sound is abundant and agents can edit, the scarce thing is no longer the operation but the intention. The next DAW is an instrument that people and agents shape together: the instrument supplies the senses, the work keeps its memory, and the ear keeps the verdict.

In the 1970s and 1980s, the great recording studios were among the most expensive rooms in the world. A console the size of a car, a wall of tape machines, an echo chamber, a staff of engineers and assistants, session players on call: if you wanted a record that sounded like a record, you rented the room. Careers were made in those rooms. John Lennon, Stevie Wonder, Bruce Springsteen and Michael Jackson all worked at the Hit Factory in New York. And then, within a generation, the business was in retreat, and nobody attacked it. The Hit Factory closed in 2005, and its building became condominiums sold with the slogan “Live in the House That Rock Built.” EMI closed Olympic Studios in London in 2009 because it no longer made money, and in 2010 it put even Abbey Road up for sale, until the British government listed the building to stop developers turning it into flats. In 2020, the Grammy for Album of the Year went to a record produced in a small bedroom studio in Los Angeles, in an off-the-shelf DAW.

The mistake is to think the studios sold rooms, or tape, or consoles. They sold the finished record: the best way anyone had found to turn a musical idea into music other people could hear the way it was meant. When software served the same want far more cheaply, beginning with Pro Tools in 1991, the want quietly took the shorter road. Other forces pushed too: Manhattan rents, the collapse of record sales after file-sharing, labels in debt. But the decisive fact outlived all of them. A cheaper path to the finished record had appeared, and the consoles did not lose a fight. They were bypassed, and then redrawn, knob for knob, inside the software that replaced them.

That last detail is where this essay begins. As I argued in The Selection Pressure, the want is the constant and the means keep dying. The DAW did not abolish the studio. It shrank the studio to fit on a laptop and kept its shape. Now the DAW faces the pressure it once applied, and the contest that matters is not between applications (which workstation has the better timeline, the bigger plug-in bundle, the cleverer AI button). It is over how far a person must travel between a musical intention and its realization, and what carries them across.

Imagine hearing a song and wishing the bass would wait a little longer before it returns. The intention takes a second to form. Realizing it can take an evening: find the source, separate the parts, learn an editor, rebuild the passage, render it, compare it with the original, and hope you can find the file next week. The digital audio workstation, the DAW, is the most capable musical instrument ever built, and it still charges that evening as the price of the second.

My thesis is simple. The DAW of the future will not be a better studio. It will be an instrument for shaping what you hear: a person and an agent working on the same editable music, through the same commands, while the instrument supplies the senses, the work keeps its memory, and the ear keeps the verdict.

The pressure for that change is already visible in the numbers. In 2025, an average of 106,000 new tracks were delivered to streaming services every day, up from 99,000 in 2024. By September 2026, Luminate’s chief executive put the figure at close to 150,000. On Deezer, fully AI-generated tracks passed half of all daily uploads in June 2026, about 90,000 a day, while drawing only 1–3% of the platform’s streams. Of the 253 million tracks on streaming services at the end of 2025, 88% were played fewer than a thousand times that year.

Making a finished-sounding track has never been cheaper. Making music that matters, first to the person who made it and then to someone else, has not become cheap at all. The next instrument will be built in that gap.

What people want from music does not change

Start with the person, not the software. Someone plays a familiar song after a difficult day. Someone else chooses it for a friend’s wedding. A third person hears a phrase and begins answering it on an instrument. The recording may be identical; the intentions differ. People use music to feel and to change how they feel, to express what they cannot easily say, to remember, to belong, to exercise taste, and sometimes to build a skill over years. These tendencies are far older than any technology, and they will outlast this one. Human wants change slowest. Objectives are more stable than actions, actions are more stable than interfaces, and implementations change fastest.

From those wants come three needs that matter for an instrument: music that fits me now; music in which I recognize my own choices; and music through which another person answers me. They can occur separately. Personalization can satisfy the first without any authorship. Private making can satisfy the second without an audience. Only the third needs another person, and only when someone actually wants to respond.

Two cautions keep this honest. Listening is a complete destination, not a failed attempt to create; the person who only wants to hear the song has lost nothing. And effort is not always a cost. People avoid the effort that stands between them and the music, but they seek the effort that is the music, such as practising a rhythm or finding a sound by ear. A good instrument removes the first kind and protects the second.

That is the measure every tool in this essay is held to. Technology changes what can be done and what it costs. It does not change what people want; it changes which of their wants have become affordable, and which costs are left standing.

The DAW is a studio you carry

For a generation, the main instrument for acting on those wants with recorded music has been the DAW, and every DAW on the market is, at heart, a recording studio drawn as software. The transport is a tape machine. The tracks are tape. The mixer is a console, the plug-ins are a rack of outboard gear, and the piano roll is a player-piano roll turned on its side. Each panel stands for a job that a person once did: tape operator, engineer, session player, sound designer, mixer.

That inheritance was a triumph. Recording made a performance repeatable. Multitrack made its parts revisable. Software put the whole construction on a personal computer. But what software collapsed was the cost of equipment, not the cost of knowing. You still have to learn the console before it will serve you, and learn the synthesizer before it will give you the sound you can already hear in your head. Affording a tool and realizing an intention are different kinds of access.

The history of music technology is a sequence of such partial victories. Notation let musical instructions travel without the performer, while literacy, teaching and publishers remained the gates. Recording let people hear music without its players in the room; financing, manufacture and promotion became the new gates. MIDI, agreed in 1983, let performance data travel between machines from different makers, but the agreement carried the instructions, not the music. Streaming delivered music anywhere, and listening concentrated on a small share of an enormous output. Each wave put a new power in people’s hands and left a question it could not answer.

Tools also acquire meanings their makers never planned. Roland released the TB-303 in 1981 as a bass accompaniment for guitarists. It failed at that job and was discontinued in 1984. Cheap second-hand units then became the squelching voice of Chicago acid house, beginning with Phuture’s Acid Tracks. Nobody can design a scene. The most an instrument can do is leave room for one.

A chain bought in layers

Look at what an electronic producer actually pays for, and the DAW turns out to be one link in a longer chain:

  1. the engine: the synthesizers that make sound;
  2. the content: presets, samples, loops and patterns, per genre;
  3. access: libraries that supply and search sounds on demand;
  4. know-how: lessons, and other people’s project files;
  5. assembly: turning parts into a track, alone or with others;
  6. release: labels, streaming services and direct sales;
  7. performance: the DJs, clubs and festivals where music meets a room.

A different seller supplies each layer, and each layer is someone’s livelihood: instrument makers, sound designers, pack makers, tutors, engineers. Each layer is also a separate thing to learn. The producer pays twice for every layer: once in money, and once in learning.

The DAW sits at the assembly layer. It is the workbench where parts bought elsewhere are put together, which is why it has been so stable for so long. It is the centre of a chain whose other links it does not own.

Integration alone is not the answer, and neither is price. Free, integrated workstations already exist at scale: BandLab, a free studio for the browser and the phone with loops and collaboration built in, passed 100 million users in 2024. Free and integrated are not new. What would be new is an instrument that joins the layers and acts on the work itself.

When sound is abundant, scarcity moves

When one barrier falls, another comes into view. Recording reduced the need for performers in the room, and distribution and catalogue control became the bottleneck. Personal software removed most of the equipment barrier; learning and judgment remained. Streaming made delivery nearly free, and attention became the scarce thing. The central claim of The Selection Pressure is that when one cost falls, the rent relocates to whichever cost has not yet fallen. Music is now running that experiment at full speed.

Generative models have made starting material abundant. The upload figures above are what abundance looks like, and they carry a warning. Cheaper production does not make sources cheaper to obtain. Easier delivery does not bring an audience. More output does not show that any of it matters to anyone. Deezer estimates that up to 85% of the streams that fully AI-generated tracks received in 2025 were fraudulent.

If sound is no longer scarce, what is? For a person trying to realize a musical intention, four things remain expensive:

  • Understanding and control: reaching the part you mean, knowing what a choice will do, and hearing whether it helped.
  • Learning and attention: gaining a capability without spending more effort on setup and evaluation than the outcome is worth.
  • Reliable material: finding, preparing and keeping the sounds the work depends on.
  • Continuity and trust: returning to the work, tracing where each piece came from, and handing it to someone else in a state they can continue.

These are not features. They are where the cost now sits. The next instrument will be judged by how much of that cost it removes, without removing the effort people actually want to spend: practising a rhythm, choosing the take, finding the sound by ear.

The prompt is not an instrument

The most visible answer to abundance is the song generator: type a description, receive a finished track. It is a remarkable technology, and the record industry has decided to live with it. Universal Music settled its lawsuit against Udio in October 2025 and agreed to build a licensed service with it. Warner Music settled with Suno a month later and announced a partnership. Prompt-to-song is being absorbed into the old chain as a catalogue business.

That settles a legal question. It does not settle a musical one.

A prompt steers only approximately. Words fall short of what a person hears: “a warmer bass” describes a thousand basses, and people usually find the one they meant by hearing it. The deeper problem is exactness. A generated recording is accepted or rejected whole. You cannot keep the melody you love, change the rhythm under it, and then nudge a single hi-hat, without asking the machine to roll the dice again and hoping it remembers what you liked.

Generation becomes part of production only when it has an editable target to generate into: a project that the person and the agent can both open, hear and change. A generated sound should arrive as material, something you can keep, edit within what it supports, replace or build on, rather than as a sealed object. Recordings remain precious, and sampling culture is built on them. But a recording is something you listen to. A project is something you continue.

Programming went first

Software development has already been through the transition music is entering. For decades, code was written in an editor, built by a compiler and explained by a manual: three separate tools, each learned separately. Within a few years the work became integrated and assisted. A developer states an objective; an assistant reads the codebase, changes the files directly and runs the tests; version control records every change with its author; the developer reviews, keeps or reverts. In Stack Overflow’s 2025 survey, 84% of developers were using or planning to use AI tools, and about half of professional developers used them every day.

What made this work was not the chat window. It was four quieter things: a shared editable object (the repository), the same commands for the person and the agent (the agent edits files like any other editor), senses (compilers, tests and logs that tell the agent what actually happened), and memory (version control, so nothing is lost and every change has a name).

“Cursor for music”, after the AI code editor, is shorthand for the same relationship. Bring the bass back four bars later and leave the vocal alone. That one sentence names a change and what must survive it. The agent makes the change in the song itself; the person hears it in context, compares it with the version before, and keeps or reverts it.

The analogy breaks at exactly one point, and it is the most important point in this essay. Tests can check code. Nothing can check taste. A measurement can say whether a kick lands on the beat, whether a mix clips, or whether a tail decays. It cannot say whether a phrase says what you meant. In music, the test suite is a human ear.

What the next DAW must do

Five properties follow from that starting point. None of them is a feature list; each describes a relationship between a person, an agent and the music.

Start from the intention, at every depth

People think about music in musical terms: hear the whole, attend to a part, change a sound, hear the result in context. The instrument should let them work at whatever depth the intention lives (“let the chorus arrive later” is arrangement; “a warmer bass” is sound) and at whatever depth the material honestly supports. A recording does not automatically yield clean, independent parts, and the instrument should say so rather than pretend.

Zooming in should change the precision of control, not the person’s right to direct it. A first useful action should be reachable without learning the whole tool. A beginner may stop after one change; an expert may need one note in one bar. Both should be served by the same work, and returning to the broad view must never flatten the detailed decisions already made. A low floor, never a low ceiling.

Let people and agents edit the same song

In the next DAW, an agent is not a separate app that sends back a file. It edits the song directly, like any other editor, within the access the person grants, whether that is one change or a whole task such as a kit for a fast, hard track or a sixteen-bar intro. Every operation the agent performs is one the person could perform by hand, so the agent’s work can also teach.

There is no private project that only the agent can see, no change that exists in the conversation but not in the music, and no second door: no approval queue that an agent’s change must pass and the person’s own change need not. Close the conversation, and every change, every version and every control stays exactly where it was.

The agent does not need to be a new brain, either. The general agents people already use are improving every month without anyone’s help. What they lack is the instrument: its operations, its senses, the person’s taste in a form they can act on, and models built from the person’s own material. A musical agent’s job is to equip the general agents, not to compete with them.

Hear for the agent

An assistant that cannot perceive the music will invent it: a confident description of a clip that says nothing true, a key guessed from a filename, a new layer that fights the kick. Agents do not hear, so the instrument must hear for them.

That means typed musical evidence produced by the instrument itself, not imagined by a model: the key as a ranked distribution rather than a single guessed label, beat grids with their confidence, energy and brightness over time, loudness, and whether a new part would fill an open pocket or clash with what is already there. When the evidence is ambiguous, the agent should receive the ambiguity. Two different assistants on two different days should read the same truth about the same audio. The agent’s judgment may vary; the evidence must not.

The same holds for sound itself. For an agent, the text written about a sound serves as its eyes and ears: the sound’s settings in human units (seconds, hertz, decibels), where it sits among its neighbours, and how its render actually measured. With that, an agent can choose a sound for a role and say why, propose a change, predict what it will do, and check the prediction against the next reading. It can also say when it does not know.

Make every sound finished and editable

A producer who wants an industrial bass for a hard techno track wants that bass, finished. Samples will keep that role. What changes is what stands behind the sample. When a sound carries its editable source, it can be re-keyed, re-timed or re-shaped when the song’s needs change, instead of being searched for all over again. The sample, the preset and the lesson become one object: a finished sound, the settings that make it, and an explanation an agent can give.

The cleanest way there is a single generator for every native sound, so that a kick, a clap, a bass and a pad are regions of one space rather than four separate synthesizers. A producer with a kick they love can then start a clap from the kick’s own settings, in controls they already know. Patterns play sounds by reference: swap the kick, and the groove survives with its timing and velocities intact. Every sound the generator makes becomes a lesson that transfers, for the person and for the agent. Whether one space can match instruments built for a single kind of sound is a question the ear must settle, kind by kind.

Remember the work

Programmers take version control for granted; musicians mostly do without it. The next DAW should record every change with its maker, whether the person, a collaborator or an agent, and let anyone allowed to edit keep a version, the way a commit keeps code. Any version can be heard, compared with any other and returned to, and nothing kept is lost by going back.

Memory is what makes experimentation safe: you can let an agent try three endings because the one you had is never at risk. It is also what makes music continuable. Imagine publishing a piece together with its arrangement and its sounds. Someone hears it, enters the drum part, tries a different rhythm and keeps a descendant. The original stays intact, and its maker can hear the answer and continue from it in turn. A work becomes an invitation to a musical answer, and a good answer becomes another beginning.

None of this is compulsory. Listening is complete on its own, and a private version for the journey home is a perfectly good destination. Sharing is an act the person chooses.

The verdict stays with the ear

Who is the author of a song made with an agent? The most honest answer is the one programming already uses. The person who directs the work, who writes the brief, guides each pass and decides what stays, is its author. The record keeps what an agent or a generator contributed distinguishable, so the history tells the truth about how the music was made. Directing an agent is a form of expression, not the absence of one.

Music is music, whether a person made it by hand, with an agent or in any tool. What matters is access to its building blocks, the sounds, the parts and how they are arranged, because with those a person can keep hearing, changing and building on it.

What must never move is the verdict. Readings are not ears. A loudness, a pitch and a brightness describe a sound; whether it is a kick you would use is your answer, given after listening. An agent may judge its own attempts, and it should say which ones it set aside. It may not claim to have heard the music, and it may not put its choice in place of yours.

What could prove this wrong

This is a thesis, not a prophecy, and it faces serious objections. Each one can be tested.

Most people may simply want to listen. Listening is a complete musical act, not an unfinished step on the way to making something. If people offered an easy way to change a song mostly go on listening, the demand for shaping is a minority one, and the instrument must be honest about that.

Participation can be attractive and still lose. Player-piano sales in America peaked in 1924; within a few years the electrical phonograph and radio had swept them aside, and the crash of 1929 ended their prominence. Many buyers seem to have valued the convenient outcome more than the act of steering. The question for any new instrument is whether people return for the act itself, or only because it is, for now, the easiest way to get a result.

The incumbents can absorb it. Free, integrated studios already reach enormous numbers of people, and the established DAWs can bring assistants and sample libraries inside. Integration and price prove nothing by themselves. A new instrument must be clearly better at something people do often.

Review may cost more than it saves. An agent that works while you are away can leave you with more changes than you can hear. If people stop listening to what agents change, the agents’ reach must narrow.

Recorded character may win. A recording carries performance, room and history that synthesis may never reach. Offered an editable and a frozen version of the same part, producers may choose the frozen one. Then recordings must stand beside editable sounds as first-class material.

Consent and displacement are unresolved. New instruments have always met organized objection from the players they displaced; synthesizers did too. Assistance that can make whole songs raises that question again, together with the consent of the musicians whose recordings trained the models. In June 2026 the American Federation of Musicians sued Universal and Warner, alleging that recordings made by its members were licensed to Suno and Udio without compensation or credit. These questions do not have engineering answers.

The order of change

The intuitive order of change is:

  1. first, a new kind of DAW;
  2. then, new habits around it.

The likelier order is the reverse:

  1. first, agents arrive inside the work people already have, including inside the workstations they already use, bringing better senses than those workstations have;
  2. then the editable project, not the finished recording, becomes the object people and agents share, keep and continue;
  3. and only later, if at all, a new instrument replaces the studio metaphor.

Software followed the same sequence: assistants entered the editors developers already used well before anyone argued for a new kind of editor.

The studio metaphor will not end the day an alternative becomes better at everything. It will begin to end the day an alternative becomes better at the thing people do most: changing a song and coming back to it.

The instrument of the past and the future

This is the instrument I am building. It is called Dombra, after the two-stringed lute of the Kazakh steppe, a living instrument that has carried songs and stories for centuries. The name holds the ambition: an instrument of the past and the future, in which a gesture becomes sound now and the work survives to be continued. Its mark is two strings across three bands of colour, red for the lows, green for the mids and violet for the highs, with the low band longest, because low frequencies travel furthest. That is why the kick is what you still hear outside a club. Its musical agent is called Seyre, after a singing bird. Seyre does not try to out-think the general agents; it gives them the instrument’s hands and ears, and the person’s taste in a form they can act on.

Every decision in Dombra answers one question: how does this improve human expression, or help someone realize their musical intention? Whose intention, what becomes better, what stays under their judgment, and what evidence would show that it worked.

The DAW of the past was a studio you had to learn before it would serve you. The DAW of the future meets you at your intention, lets an agent carry the work you do not want to carry, keeps every choice you might want back, and leaves the last word to your ear.

The studio is still standing. But the first versions of its successor are already on the workbench.
